THE BIG STORY

Thousands of possible portfolios, one boundary worth holding

Plot every combination of assets by its risk (σp) and expected return. Some are clearly worse than others: same risk, less return. The efficient frontier is the leftmost boundary of that feasible set, the best attainable trade-offs. Which point on it you choose depends on you.

Reading Figure 5.7

Feasible (attainable) set

All the portfolios that can be built from the available assets.

Portfolios to the right of the frontier are inferior: another attainable portfolio offers the same return with less risk, or more return for the same risk.

The efficient frontier

The leftmost boundary of the feasible set, containing all efficient portfolios. Portfolios to its left are not available.

Technically, it is the upper part of the minimum-variance boundary. The goal is not "minimise σ" but "minimise risk for the desired return, or maximise return for acceptable risk".

Utility (indifference) curves

Curves showing the investor's willingness to trade more risk for more return.

The best portfolio for that investor is where the highest utility curve touches the frontier. A retiree may pick 8% / 7% σ; a younger investor 13% / 18% σ. Both can be efficient.

Two graphs that are often confused

Efficient frontier: x = portfolio σ, a portfolio-construction tool (MPT). SML: x = beta, a pricing benchmark (CAPM).

Traditional portfolio management

  • "Balance" the portfolio with a wide variety of stocks and bonds
  • A broad range of industries
  • Familiar, well-known companies: seen as less risky, more liquid, more comfortable
  • Limitation: categories and judgment, not measured co-movement

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT)

  • Statistical measures: expected returns, standard deviations, correlations
  • Combines securities with negative or low positive correlations
  • Diversification becomes measurable and optimisable
  • Limitation: only as good as the estimates; correlations change

Reconciling the two: the chapter's recommended policy

Decide how much risk you are willing to bear; diversify across different types of securities and industries; pay attention to the correlation of returns; use beta to keep the portfolio at an acceptable level of risk; and evaluate alternatives to pick the highest return for that risk.

Traditional judgment (practicality, liquidity) + MPT discipline (correlations, beta) > either alone

EVALUATE

"I'll hold a portfolio below the frontier because it feels safer."

Agree?

It confuses comfort with efficiency. A cautious investor should choose a lower-risk point on the frontier, not a dominated portfolio below it.
ANALYSE

Do all investors pick the same efficient portfolio?

Why or why not?

No. The frontier lists the efficient choices; each investor's risk preference (utility curve) selects among them.
CREATE

Design a process for managing RM100,000

Outline the steps.

Define the objective → set risk capacity and tolerance → estimate returns → measure σ, correlations and beta → construct and compare portfolios → test practicality (costs, liquidity, currency, unstable correlations) → choose a near-efficient portfolio and monitor.

Exam lens

  • Frontier = best attainable risk-return trade-offs; to its right = inferior; to its left = unattainable.
  • The optimal portfolio depends on investor preferences (utility curves).
  • Traditional vs MPT: be ready to compare them and explain why the chapter recommends combining them.

Quick check

A mathematically optimal portfolio relies on one illiquid, very expensive-to-trade asset. Should you hold it?

Not automatically. MPT gives quantitative discipline, but traditional practicality (liquidity, costs) still matters; a slightly less "optimal" but robust portfolio can be the better choice.
