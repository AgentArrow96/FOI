THE BIG STORY

RM5,000 of your own money controls RM10,000 of stock

You put in RM5,000, borrow RM5,000 from your broker, and buy RM10,000 of shares. If the shares rise 20%, your equity rises 40%. If they fall 20%, your equity falls 40%. Margin trading means using borrowed funds to buy securities, with the securities as collateral. Leverage does not improve the investment; it amplifies your exposure.

1Long purchaseBuy and hold, hoping the price rises: "buy low, sell high".
2Margin purchaseThe same bet, partly with borrowed money: gains and losses magnified.
3Short saleBet the price falls: sell borrowed shares, buy back later (next lesson).
4The common threadMargin means you owe money; shorting means you owe shares.

Margin is NOT the amount borrowed

Margin is the share of the position's current value that belongs to you: your equity divided by the market value.

Margin = (V − D) / V = investor equity / market value (V = value of securities, D = debit balance or loan)

Worked numbers

RM10,000 position, RM5,000 equity, price +20%

Value → RM12,000, profit RM2,000. Unlevered: 2,000 / 10,000 = 20%. With margin: 2,000 / 5,000 = 40% (before borrowing costs).

Smaller equity base ⇒ bigger percentage effect.

V = RM10,000, D = RM4,000 (margin 60%), price falls 30%

V → RM7,000, D still RM4,000, equity → RM3,000. Margin = 3,000 / 7,000 = 42.86%.

The stock fell 30% but your equity fell (6,000 − 3,000) / 6,000 = 50%. The debt did not absorb the loss; your equity did.

Deriving the margin-call price (an extension of the slide formula)

From $m = (V-D)/V$: $D = V(1-m)$, so $V^* = \dfrac{D}{1-m}$ and $P^* = \dfrac{D}{N(1-m)}$.

Buy 100 shares at RM50 with 50% initial margin: D = RM2,500. With a 30% maintenance margin, $V^* = 2{,}500 / 0.70 \approx 3{,}571.43$ ringgit, so the call comes at about RM35.71 a share.

The denominator is your equity

Return on invested capital = (income − interest paid + sale value − purchase value) / equity at purchase.

Interest on the margin loan is in the formula: leverage increases exposure, not free wealth.

ANALYSE

A stock falls 20%. A paid cash; B used 50% margin.

Compare their equity losses, ignoring interest.

A loses about 20%; B loses about 40% of equity, because the loan stays fixed while the whole fall is absorbed by a smaller equity base.
EVALUATE

"Margin increases return."

Improve this statement.

Margin magnifies returns relative to equity, in both directions. Favourable moves give larger percentage gains, adverse moves larger losses, and interest costs and margin-call risk reduce the attraction further.
EVALUATE

"I'll use maximum leverage: I'm sure the stock will rise."

What's the flaw?

Confidence does not remove uncertainty. Leverage makes equity more sensitive to forecast errors, adds financing cost and creates margin-call risk: a correct long-term view can still end in a forced sale after a short-term fall.

Exam lens

  • Margin = (V − D) / V; it falls as the price falls even though the loan is unchanged.
  • Returns under margin are measured on initial equity, after interest.
  • Without a stated maintenance margin, a big loss alone does not prove a margin call.

Quick check

V = RM8,000 and the loan is RM5,000. What is the margin? Is there a margin call at a 30% maintenance requirement?

(8,000 − 5,000) / 8,000 = 37.5%: above 30%, so no call yet. The call value is 5,000 / 0.70 = RM7,142.86.
