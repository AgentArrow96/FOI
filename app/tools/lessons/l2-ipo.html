THE BIG STORY

TechCo wants RM500 million and has never sold shares to the public

An initial public offering (IPO) is the first public sale of a company's stock: a primary-market transaction. Going public sounds like "decide how many shares, then sell them", but the company must answer hard questions: what is it worth, what price, who will buy, what must investors be told, and what if demand is too weak? That is why investment banks exist.

The IPO process: prepare, disclose, market, price, sell

Underwriting: transferring distribution risk

The investment bank purchases the issue at an agreed price and bears the risk of reselling it to the public.

The company does not want to discover at the last minute that nobody wants its RM1 billion issue. The underwriter takes on that risk.

Prospectus: reducing information asymmetry

The registration statement describing the issue and the issuer.

Insiders know far more than outside investors. Standardised disclosure cannot remove that asymmetry, but it reduces it.

Red herring and quiet period

Red herring: the preliminary prospectus available during the waiting period. Quiet period: after filing, the company must restrict what it says about itself.

Both control what information reaches investors before the offering.

Road show: discovering the price

A series of presentations to potential investors.

Institutions effectively reveal "at this valuation we're interested" or "at that price we're not", so marketing also helps set the offering price.

Who does what in a large issue

Lead investment bankerOrganises the deal and forms the syndicate.
Underwriting syndicateA group of banks that share the financial risk of underwriting.
Selling groupOther brokerage firms that help sell the issue to the public: wider distribution.
TombstoneThe public announcement of the issue and the firms involved.

Large issue ⇒ risk sharing (syndicate) + wider distribution (selling group).

How the investment bank gets paid

Usually through a discount on the sale price: the bank buys the shares from the issuer for, say, 19 and distributes them to investors at 20. The spread compensates distribution work, underwriting risk and marketing.

Bank's spread = offering price − price paid to the issuer

IPO vs RESALE

What separates an IPO from buying the same shares a year later?

Name the market and who gets the money.

An IPO is a primary-market sale: new ownership claims are created and the company receives the capital (net of the underwriter's spread). The later purchase is secondary: the selling investor gets the money.
WHY A SYNDICATE

Why not let one bank underwrite a huge issue alone?

Think about exposure.

One bank holding the entire unsold risk would be heavily exposed. A syndicate shares that underwriting risk; a selling group adds distribution reach.
TRAP

"An IPO simply means the stock starts trading."

What's missing?

An IPO creates and distributes new ownership claims to public investors in exchange for capital. Trading afterwards happens in the secondary market.

Exam lens

  • Underwriting = the bank buys the issue and bears the resale risk.
  • Prospectus = registration statement; red herring = preliminary prospectus; quiet period restricts statements; road show = presentations.
  • Syndicate shares risk; selling group distributes; compensation usually comes from the price discount.

Quick check

A bank agrees to buy an entire issue at RM9.40 per share and offer it to the public at RM10.00, but demand turns out weak and it sells at RM9.20. Who bears the loss, and why?

The underwriter: it already paid the issuer RM9.40, so the company received its money; the shortfall is the underwriting (distribution) risk the bank was paid to carry.
