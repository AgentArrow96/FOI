THE BIG STORY

Ammar has RM100,000 he does not need. Company X needs RM100,000 for a factory.

There is a natural match: one side has excess capital, the other has a productive opportunity but not enough capital. Investing connects them. The chapter calls the two sides suppliers of funds and demanders of funds, and the financial system is the plumbing in between.

1IndividualsTypically net suppliers of funds, even though they still borrow for houses and cars.
2BusinessesTypically net demanders: they invest in producing goods and services.
3GovernmentsTypically net demanders: federal, state and local projects and operations.
4The matchSavings flow to productive uses directly, through institutions, or through markets.

Three routes from saver to borrower

Figure 1.2 shows funds reaching demanders by three routes. Click through them.

Direct transaction

Investor → company. For example, the investor buys the company's bond straight from it.

Simple, but the saver must find the borrower, judge its creditworthiness and carry the whole risk alone.

Through an intermediary

Investor → bank, insurer or pension fund → borrower.

Why bother with a middleman? Finding borrowers, assessing credit, diversifying, processing and monitoring would be absurdly expensive for each saver to do alone. Intermediation solves that coordination problem; it does not remove the borrower's default risk.

Through financial markets

Investor → financial market → company. Money markets handle short-term funds; capital markets handle long-term funds.

Markets let many investors buy securities issued by many demanders, and Chapter 2 shows how they also let investors resell them.

Individual investors

  • Invest their own money for personal goals: retirement, a house
  • An individual managing RM50 million is still an individual investor

Institutional investors

  • Paid to manage other people's money; trade large volumes
  • Banks, life insurers, mutual funds, pension funds
  • The real distinction: whose objectives are being optimised?

The investment menu

The chapter's main categories, and what each one is really for.

Short-term investments

Lives of a year or less, high liquidity. Think "capital parking", not wealth creation.

Common stock

Corporate ownership; return = dividends + capital gains.

Fixed-income securities

Bonds, convertibles, preferred stock: more predictable, often contractual cash flows.

Mutual funds

Pool many investors' money into one portfolio, so small investors can diversify.

ETFs

The same pooling idea, but the shares trade on an exchange whenever the market is open.

Hedge funds, derivatives, other

Hedge funds pool money with higher minimums and lighter regulation; options and futures; real estate and tangibles.

Why pooling matters: diversification

RM1,000 alone might buy meaningful exposure to just one company, so you depend on that one company's fate. Through a fund, the same RM1,000 gives exposure to hundreds of companies, and one company failing becomes far less catastrophic. The ETF changes the trading structure, not the pooling idea.

Diversification = avoiding unnecessary concentration of risk (Chapter 5 makes this mathematical)
ROLE

A pension fund buys shares for employees' retirement

Individual or institutional investor?

Institutional: it manages money on behalf of others. That the ultimate beneficiaries are people does not make it an individual investor.
FUNCTION

A bank takes household deposits and lends to a manufacturer

What economic function is this?

Financial intermediation: channelling funds from suppliers (households) to demanders (the business). It does not eliminate the borrower's default risk.
TRAP

"A fund owns hundreds of stocks, so it is risk-free."

True or false?

False. Diversification reduces certain risks (one company failing) but cannot guarantee safety: a broad market fall still hits the whole fund. You also own fund units, not the underlying shares directly.

Exam lens

  • Individuals are typically net suppliers; businesses and governments are typically net demanders.
  • Institutions exist because they make screening, pooling, diversification and monitoring cheap, not because "banks sit in the middle".
  • Mutual fund vs ETF: same pooling idea; ETFs trade on exchanges during market hours.

Quick check

Why might a saver prefer to put money in a bank rather than lend directly to a company, even if the direct loan pays a higher rate?

The intermediary screens borrowers, pools and diversifies loans and monitors them, tasks that would be expensive and risky for one saver. The lower rate partly pays for that service and for the reduced concentration of risk.
