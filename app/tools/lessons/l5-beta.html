THE BIG STORY

A CEO quits; interest rates spike: two very different shocks

When Company A's CEO leaves unexpectedly, A's share price falls, but B, C and D are unaffected; in a diversified portfolio that shock is diluted. When rates spike and the economy slides into recession, many companies fall together, and owning more stocks does not help. Total risk splits into these two parts, and beta measures the second.

Two components of risk

Diversifiable (unsystematic) risk comes from firm-specific, uncontrollable or random events such as labour strikes and lawsuits, and can be eliminated through diversification. Nondiversifiable (systematic) risk comes from forces affecting all similar investments, such as war, inflation and political events, and cannot.

Total risk = nondiversifiable (systematic) risk + diversifiable (unsystematic) risk

Beta: sensitivity to market movements

A measure of undiversifiable risk

How a security's price responds to market forces, comparing its historical returns with a market benchmark such as the S&P 500. The market's beta is 1.0.

Betas can be positive or negative (nearly all are positive). Above 1.0: more responsive than the market. Below 1.0: less.

Approximate market-linked moves

Market +10% and β = 1.5 → about +15%. Market −8% and β = 0.5 → about −4%.

2.0 = twice the market; 0.5 = half; 0 = unaffected; −1.0 = opposite. Beta is an estimated linear relationship, not a guarantee: a β 1.5 stock can still fall in a rising market on company news.

Where beta comes from (an extension beyond the slides)

$\beta_i = \dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(R_m)} = \rho_{i,m}\dfrac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}$

ρ = 0.75, σi = 30%, σm = 15% → β = 0.75 × 2 = 1.5. You cannot get beta from the stock's σ alone.

Different questions

σ asks: how much does the return fluctuate? Beta asks: how sensitive is it to the market?

A stock driven by company news might have σ = 40% but β = 0.5; another σ = 25% but β = 1.3. Neither measure replaces the other.

Portfolio beta is a weighted average

$\beta_p = \sum w_i\beta_i$. The Austin Fund's portfolios (Table 5.6):

Portfolio V0.10(1.65) + 0.30(1.00) + 0.20(1.30) + 0.20(1.10) + 0.20(1.25) = 1.195
Portfolio W0.10(0.80) + 0.10(1.00) + 0.20(0.65) + 0.10(0.75) + 0.50(1.05) = 0.910

A 10-point market rise implies about +11.95 points for V and +9.10 for W: sensitivities, not guaranteed returns. Beta alone tells you nothing about the portfolios' total σ.

EVALUATE

"A β 0.5 stock is safer than a β 1.5 stock."

A: β 0.5, σ 45%. B: β 1.5, σ 30%.

A has lower market exposure but higher total volatility. It can't be declared unconditionally safer from beta alone; say which risk you mean.
EVALUATE

"Diversification eliminates risk."

Agree?

No. It mainly removes unsystematic risk; systematic risk remains, and in a crisis correlations among risky assets can rise. It improves risk management, it doesn't prevent losses.
CALCULATE

40% in a β 1.2 stock, 60% in a β 0.7 stock

Portfolio beta?

0.4 × 1.2 + 0.6 × 0.7 = 0.48 + 0.42 = 0.90: slightly less market-sensitive than the market.

Exam lens

  • Unsystematic: firm-specific, diversifiable. Systematic: market-wide, not diversifiable.
  • Market beta = 1.0; β is the measure of undiversifiable risk.
  • Total volatility (σ) ≠ market sensitivity (β). Portfolio β = weighted average.

Quick check

The market is expected to fall 10%. Roughly what do stocks with β = 2.0 and β = −1.0 do?

β = 2.0: about −20%. β = −1.0: about +10%, moving opposite to the market (estimates, not guarantees).
