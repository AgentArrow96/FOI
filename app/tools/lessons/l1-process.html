THE BIG STORY

RM20,000 in stocks, no emergency fund, and a sudden RM10,000 bill

Six months after investing everything, the market is down 35% and the investor must sell. The problem was not necessarily picking bad stocks; it was a mismatch between the asset and the investor's circumstances. That is why the chapter's seven steps put prerequisites and goals before choosing any product.

The seven steps, and the logic behind each

Don't memorise seven bureaucratic steps. Compress them: survive → define → plan → analyse → select → diversify → monitor.

Step 1: meet the investment prerequisites

Provide for the necessities of life, including funds for emergency cash needs, and protect against common risks such as death, illness and disability.

This is "survive" first. Suitability depends not only on what will earn the most but on what you can afford to hold through adverse conditions.

Step 2: establish investment goals

Examples: accumulating retirement funds, enhancing income, saving for a major expenditure, sheltering income from taxes.

The product should follow the goal, never "cool investment first, justification later". The same equity fund can suit a 35-year retirement goal and be wrong for university fees due in six months.

Step 3: adopt an investment plan

Write the plan down and specify a target date and a risk tolerance for each goal.

Goal identification (step 2) and planning (step 3) are different steps; MCQs like to blur them.

Steps 4–5: evaluate return and risk, then select

Assess the potential return and risk of candidate investments (Chapter 4 covers risk in detail), research them, then make the selections.

Selection comes only after the investor's needs are clear; that is the whole point of the order.

Steps 6–7: build a diversified portfolio and manage it

Use a portfolio of different investments (diversification can increase returns or reduce risk), then compare actual behaviour with expected performance and take corrective action when needed.

Step 7 makes investing a feedback loop. Monitoring is not panic-selling after a 5% dip; it asks whether the investment thesis or the investor's circumstances have materially changed.

Investor A: university fees in 6 months

  • Short horizon, high liquidity need
  • Cannot absorb a large fall in principal
  • Equities: unsuitable for this goal

Investor B: retirement in 35 years

  • Long horizon, time to recover from falls
  • Can hold through volatility
  • Equities: potentially suitable

Suitability is a function, not a product

Same asset, different answer, because suitability depends on the investor. That idea runs through every remaining chapter.

Suitability = f(objective, time horizon, risk, liquidity needs)

ANALYSE

Ahmad: house purchase in nine months

His friend says: put all RM100,000 into a high-growth tech ETF, it has a great track record.

Goal: house. Horizon: 9 months. Liquidity need: very high. Ability to absorb a capital loss: very low. The ETF can be sold tomorrow (market liquidity) but not necessarily at the price paid: market liquidity ≠ price stability. Financially unsuitable.
ORDER

Which step comes first?

An investor has no emergency fund but has picked three stocks to buy.

Step 1, meeting prerequisites (emergency funds, insurance), must come before selecting investments. Otherwise a cash need can force a sale at the worst moment.
CREATE

Two goals, one investor

A 35-year-old saves for a house deposit in 2 years and retirement in 30.

Separate the goals: a house fund in short-term, liquid, low-volatility assets, and a retirement fund that is long-term and growth-oriented. One investor can need two strategies.

Exam lens

  • Know the seven steps in order, and explain why prerequisites and goals come first.
  • Step 7 (manage) = compare actual vs expected performance and take corrective action: a control loop.
  • Answer suitability questions with the investor's goal, horizon, liquidity need and capacity for loss, not with "stocks are risky".

Quick check

A stock in a long-term portfolio falls 5% in a week. According to step 7, what should the investor do?

Compare actual with expected performance and ask whether the investment thesis or the investor's circumstances have materially changed. A small price move alone is not a reason for corrective action.
