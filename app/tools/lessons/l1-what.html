THE BIG STORY

A condominium, a gold bar and a share: which ones are investments?

It depends on why you hold them. The chapter defines an investment as any asset into which funds are placed with the expectation that it will generate positive income and/or an increase in value. A house you live in is consumption; the same house rented out is an investment; a house you live in while renting out rooms is both. The object does not decide. The economic purpose does.

Return has two sources

The chapter defines return as the reward for owning an investment, and it comes from two places: cash you receive while you hold it (rent, dividends, interest) and the change in what the asset is worth. Keep both in view from the very first chapter; Chapter 4 turns this into a formula.

Investment return = cash received (income) + change in value (capital gain or loss)

Debt, equity or derivative? Look at the contract

The most important classification is the economic claim you hold. Click through each one.

Debt: "I will lend you money"

The investor lends funds in exchange for interest income and the promised repayment of the loan. Example: bonds.

You are a creditor. The issuer has a contractual promise to pay interest and repay principal, so your upside is capped at that promise, but you rank ahead of owners if things go wrong.

Equity: "I want part of the business"

An ongoing ownership interest in a business or property. Example: common stock.

You are an owner. Nobody promises that your investment will grow; your payoff depends on how the business performs, through dividends and a rising (or falling) share price.

Derivative: value borrowed from something else

Neither debt nor equity: a security whose value is derived from an underlying asset such as a stock or a commodity. Examples: options and futures.

The stock's value comes from the company; the option's value comes from the stock. That second-order relationship is what makes derivatives different: an option holder is not automatically a shareholder.

What most people think risk means

"Risk means losing money."

  • Only describes one bad outcome
  • Ignores outcomes better than expected
  • Leads to "low risk = good, high risk = bad"

What the chapter means

Risk is the uncertainty surrounding the return: the chance that the actual outcome differs from the expected one.

  • Investment A: expected 5%, realistic range 4.8%–5.2%
  • Investment B: expected 12%, realistic range −30% to +50%
  • B is not "bad"; it is more uncertain. Always ask: at what risk?

TRAP

"My condo is not an investment because I live in it."

The owner lives in one room and rents out the others.

Wrong. An investment is defined by the expectation of income and/or appreciation. The condo supplies housing and rental income at the same time: consumption and investment are not mutually exclusive, and appreciation is not required.

Exam lens

  • Define an investment by its purpose (income and/or growth in value), never by the object.
  • Classify along several dimensions at once; MCQs often offer a "right" answer that gets one dimension wrong.
  • Debt = creditor, equity = owner, derivative = value derived from an underlying asset.
  • Risk = uncertainty of the outcome. A higher expected return alone never proves an investment is better.

Quick check

An investor buys an option whose value moves with a company's share price. Why is it a derivative, and does the investor now own part of the company?

It is a derivative because its value is derived from an underlying asset (the share). It gives no ongoing ownership of the company: the option holder is not automatically a shareholder.
