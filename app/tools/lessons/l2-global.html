THE BIG STORY

A U.S. stock rises 10%, and a Malaysian investor loses money

If the U.S. dollar falls 12% against the ringgit over the same period, the investor's home-currency return is $(1.10)(0.88) - 1 = -3.2\%$. International investing brings genuine diversification benefits, but it also brings new risks. And every market, domestic or foreign, needs rules to stay trustworthy.

Why invest internationally

  • More industries and securities to choose from
  • Securities denominated in different currencies
  • Opportunities in rapidly expanding economies
  • Foreign markets do not necessarily move with the home market: that is where diversification comes from

What it adds

  • Government-policy risk: unstable governments, different laws on trade, labour and taxation
  • Currency exchange-rate risk
  • Foreign markets can be riskier and less tightly regulated
  • The usual investment risks still apply

The currency arithmetic

What matters to a Malaysian investor is the return in ringgit: the asset's return in its own currency compounded with the currency's move.

(1 + R_home) = (1 + R_foreign asset) × (1 + R_currency) e.g. (1.10)(0.88) − 1 = −3.2%

Ways to invest abroad

IndirectBuy shares of home-market multinationals with large foreign operations.
Direct, on foreign exchangesBuy securities listed abroad.
Foreign firms listed at homeBuy foreign companies' securities traded on the home exchange.
ADRsDollar-denominated receipts for foreign shares held by banks. Currency risk does not vanish just because the receipt is in dollars.

Regulation: what problem does each law solve?

Don't memorise a graveyard of dates. Each rule targets information asymmetry, fraud, conflicts of interest, manipulation or unfair informational advantage.

Disclosure and a regulator

Securities Act of 1933: full disclosure of information by companies issuing securities. Securities Exchange Act of 1934: established the SEC.

Self-regulation, funds and advisers

Maloney Act of 1938: self-regulation of the securities industry (NASD). Investment Company Act of 1940: mutual funds. Investment Advisors Act of 1940: advisers must disclose and register.

Costs, insiders and accounting

1975 amendments: abolished fixed commissions. Insider Trading and Fraud Act of 1988: prohibits trading on non-public information. Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002: tighter accounting and audit rules after corporate fraud.

Why insider trading damages a market

Using non-public information about a company to make profitable securities transactions.

If investors believe the game is rigged in favour of insiders, trust falls and so does willingness to participate. Regulation preserves confidence in the market mechanism; it does not guarantee returns.

EVALUATE

"International diversification automatically lowers risk."

Agree or disagree?

Too simple. It can reduce risk when foreign markets don't move with the home market, but it also introduces currency, political and regulatory risk. It can reduce some risks while adding others.
CALCULATE

A foreign stock gains 8% and the foreign currency rises 5% against the ringgit

Return in ringgit?

(1.08)(1.05) − 1 = 13.4%. Here the currency move helps; it could just as easily hurt.
EVALUATE

"Regulation guarantees my investment returns."

Agree or disagree?

No. Regulation addresses disclosure, fraud and misconduct. Securities still fall for legitimate reasons: business performance, the economy, changing expectations.

Exam lens

  • Foreign asset return ≠ domestic investor return: always adjust for the currency.
  • Know each law by the problem it solves; the textbook's U.S. framework and trading hours are course material, not current Malaysian rules.
  • Regular U.S. trading: 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern, with extended-hours electronic sessions.

Quick check

What failure in the market was the Securities Act of 1933 mainly designed to fix?

Information asymmetry: it requires full disclosure of information by companies issuing securities.
