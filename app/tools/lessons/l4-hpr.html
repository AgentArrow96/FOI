THE BIG STORY

Investment A returned 40% in one year. Investment B returned 40% over five years.

Same holding-period return, obviously not the same performance. HPR is the easy measure of total return over a period; yield (IRR) is the compound annual rate that respects the time value of money. The same arithmetic gives growth rates.

Holding period return

The total return earned from holding an investment for a specified period, usually a year or less: income plus the capital gain or loss, relative to the beginning value.

HPR = [income + (P₁ − P₀)] / P₀ e.g. buy RM100, dividend RM5, end RM108 → (5 + 8) / 100 = 13%

HPR: advantages

  • Easy to calculate and understand
  • Includes both income and growth

HPR: disadvantages

  • Ignores the time value of money
  • Can mislead when the holding period is longer than a year

Realized vs paper return

Realized returnCurrent return actually received during the period.
Paper returnA return achieved but not yet realised: no sale has taken place. Buy at RM100, now RM150: RM50 paper gain until you sell.

Yield (IRR) and growth rates

The compound annual rate

From $FV = PV(1+r)^n$: $r = \left(\dfrac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/n} - 1$.

Chapter example: 1,000 grows to 1,400 in 5 years: $1.4^{1/5} - 1 \approx$ 6.96% a year, although the total gain was 40%.

IRR for several cash flows

The discount rate that makes the present value of the income stream equal to the investment's price: $P_0 = \displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t}$.

The same TVM system as Appendix 4A, with a different unknown: now you know the price and the cash flows and solve for $r$.

Accept when IRR ≥ required return

IRR = 9% clears a 7% hurdle but fails a 12% one.

No return is "good" in isolation. IRR uses the time value of money and lets investments with different holding periods be compared; its drawback is that the calculation is complex.

The critical assumption

Using IRR assumes all income received can be reinvested at the same rate. The reinvestment rate is what you actually earn on that income; the fully compounded rate of return includes the interest earned on interest.

A 10% investment whose cash distributions can only be reinvested at 3% will build less wealth than "10% compounded" suggests. Figure 4.1 shows how big interest on interest becomes over long horizons.

Growth rate: same arithmetic, different stream

The compound annual rate of change in a stream such as dividends: $g = \left(\dfrac{D_n}{D_0}\right)^{1/n} - 1$.

Chapter example: dividends from 0.92 (2002) to 1.85 (2011), nine years: about 8.07% a year. Not (1.85 − 0.92)/0.92: that is total growth, not annual compound growth.

EVALUATE

"A 40% five-year HPR beats a 30% one-year HPR."

Agree?

Probably not: 40% over five years is about 6.96% a year versus 30% in one year. Compare compound annual yields, not HPRs over different periods.
CALCULATE

Buy at RM50, dividend RM2, worth RM55 a year later

HPR?

[2 + (55 − 50)] / 50 = 7 / 50 = 14%.
CALCULATE

RM1,000 today; RM600 at the end of years 1 and 2; required 12%

Does the IRR clear the hurdle?

PV at 12% = 600/1.12 + 600/1.12² ≈ 535.71 + 478.32 = 1,014.03 > 1,000, so the IRR is above 12% (about 13.07%): acceptable.

Exam lens

  • HPR = (income + capital gain) / beginning value; it ignores time, so it misleads beyond one year.
  • IRR = the compound annual return that equates PV of benefits with the price; accept if IRR ≥ required return.
  • IRR assumes reinvestment at the same rate; growth rates use the same compound-rate formula.

Quick check

An asset bought for RM2,000 is worth RM3,000 after 4 years with no income. Total return and compound annual return?

HPR = 1,000 / 2,000 = 50%. Annual: 1.5^(1/4) − 1 ≈ 10.67% a year.
