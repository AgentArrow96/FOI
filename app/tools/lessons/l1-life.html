THE BIG STORY

"It's not what you make, it's what you keep"

Investment A earns 10% before tax; Investment B earns 9%. If they are taxed differently, B can leave more in the investor's pocket. This lesson collects three forces outside the investment itself that change the decision: taxes, the investor's stage of life, and the state of the economy.

{{vis:l1_real_return}}

Taxes: the conceptual framework

The chapter uses U.S. tax tables from 2012. Learn the durable ideas, not the historical percentages, unless your lecturer asks for them.

Tax planning looks at three things

The desired return after taxes · the type of income the investment produces · the timing of profit-taking and loss recognition.

So a higher pre-tax return is not necessarily the better investment.

Active, portfolio and passive income

Active: wages and salaries. Portfolio: interest, dividends, capital gains. Passive: rents, royalties, limited partnerships.

Different income sources can receive different tax treatment; that is why the type of income matters.

Capital gains and losses

A capital gain is the amount by which sale proceeds exceed the original purchase price; a capital loss is the amount by which they fall short.

In the textbook's system, capital losses can offset capital gains and timing matters. The concept travels; the specific U.S. rates do not.

{{vis:l1_lifecycle}}

Risk tolerance

The investor's psychological willingness to accept swings.

  • A 25-year-old who "hates seeing a 5% fall" has low tolerance

Risk capacity

The investor's financial ability to absorb losses: time to recover, stable income, buffers.

  • The same 25-year-old may have high capacity: decades to recover
  • Good decisions need both; the life-cycle logic is mostly about capacity

The economy around the investment

Business-cycle indicators

GDP, industrial production, disposable income and unemployment describe where the economy is.

Stocks and the cycle

Stock prices tend to rise in expansions and fall in contractions, but markets look forward, so "GDP up today, stocks up tomorrow" is too simple.

The causal chain

Economic activity → expected earnings → investor valuation → asset prices.

{{vis:l1_bond_rate}}

Why bond prices fall when interest rates rise

You own an old bond paying 5%. Rates rise and new bonds offer 8%. Nobody pays full price for your 5% bond any more, so its price must fall until its yield competes. Reverse it: if new bonds pay only 4%, your 8% bond looks attractive and its price rises. Understand the competition between old fixed cash flows and current market opportunities, not the arrow.

Interest rates ↑ ⇒ existing bond prices ↓ · Interest rates ↓ ⇒ existing bond prices ↑
EVALUATE

"Young investors should always invest aggressively."

Agree or disagree?

"Always" fails. The life-cycle framework says younger investors can typically take more risk because of longer horizons, but no emergency fund, unstable income, near-term obligations, debt or low risk tolerance can dominate. Age is a proxy for circumstances, not a rule.
APPLY

A 68-year-old retiree living partly on withdrawals

What should the portfolio emphasise?

Capital preservation and income: less time to recover from losses and current income needs. The slides list low-risk income stocks, government and quality corporate bonds and bank CDs for this stage.
ANALYSE

Market rates rise from 5% to 8%

What happens to an existing 10-year bond with a 5% coupon?

Its market price falls below face value, so that a buyer's return competes with the new 8% bonds. Its coupon payments do not change; only the rate used to value them does.

Exam lens

  • After-tax return, type of income and timing of gains are the durable tax ideas; treat the U.S. figures as historical course material.
  • Life-cycle stages: growth-oriented youth (20s–30s), middle-age consolidation (45–60), retirement (60+): explain why (risk capacity), don't just list ages.
  • Interest rates and bond prices move in opposite directions.

Quick check

Investment A returns 10% before tax and is taxed at 40%; Investment B returns 9% and is taxed at 20%. Which leaves the investor more?

A: 10% × (1 − 0.40) = 6.0% after tax. B: 9% × (1 − 0.20) = 7.2% after tax. B wins: a higher pre-tax return is not necessarily the better investment.
