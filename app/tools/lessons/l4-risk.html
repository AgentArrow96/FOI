THE BIG STORY

American Eagle averaged 26.4% a year; Target averaged 6.4%. Was American Eagle the better investment?

Not from that alone. Its returns were far more volatile: a standard deviation of about 77.2% against Target's 26.2%. The chapter defines risk as the chance that the actual return differs from what was expected. This lesson covers where risk comes from and how to measure it.

Measuring risk for a single asset

Standard deviation: absolute dispersion

$s = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (r_t - \bar r)^2}{n-1}}$: find the average, square each year's deviation (so they don't cancel), average them, take the square root.

Returns of 8, 9, 10, 11, 12% are tightly clustered; −30, 0, 10, 30, 40% average about the same but are wildly spread: higher σ, higher risk.

Coefficient of variation: risk per unit of return

$CV = \dfrac{\sigma}{\bar r}$, useful when average returns differ.

Target: 26.2 / 6.4 ≈ 4.09. American Eagle: 77.2 / 26.4 ≈ 2.92.

The two measures can disagree

American Eagle has greater absolute volatility (77.2 > 26.2) but lower volatility relative to its return (2.92 < 4.09).

Higher values of either mean higher risk, but they answer different questions.

Three attitudes to risk (Figure 4.3)

Risk-averse

Requires a higher return for greater risk: the line slopes up. The usual assumption, and it does not mean refusing all risk: "compensate me".

Risk-indifferent

Requires no change in return for more risk: a flat line.

Risk-seeking

Accepts a lower return in exchange for greater risk: the line slopes down.

The chapter's four-step decision process

Estimate expected return; assess risk with standard deviation or CV; check that the return is reasonable for the risk; select the investments with the highest expected return for the level of risk you are willing to accept.

Estimate return → measure risk → compare compensation → choose
CLASSIFY

A bond's price falls because market rates rose

Which risk?

Interest-rate risk. (If its fixed coupons buy less because prices rose, that is purchasing-power risk.)
COMPARE

A: mean 10%, σ 20%. B: mean 20%, σ 30%.

Which is riskier?

A has the lower σ (absolute risk), but CV(A) = 2.0 and CV(B) = 1.5, so B has less risk per unit of return. State which measure you are using.
EVALUATE

"B has a lower standard deviation, so B is superior."

B: E(R) 2%, σ 1%. A: E(R) 10%, σ 6%.

Incomplete. Low risk is not automatically desirable if the return is very low. The choice depends on the required return, risk tolerance and objectives: optimise the risk-return relationship, don't just minimise risk.

Exam lens

  • Know all nine risk sources with an example each; separate business from financial and event from market risk.
  • σ measures absolute dispersion; CV = σ / mean measures relative dispersion.
  • Risk-return trade-off: more risk should earn more expected return, never a guarantee.

Quick check

A hurricane damages properties insured by one company, and its share price drops sharply while the market is flat. Event risk or market risk?

Event risk: a sudden, specific event with an unusually immediate impact on that investment. Market risk comes from broad forces affecting investments generally.
